What drives how Canadians rate their health care system?

Data cleaning, assumption checks and an ordinal regression model

Key findings

This analysis aimed to investigate, which factor(s) best predict how someone may feel about the current Canadian healthcare system? To answer this, data was pulled from Open Canada https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/3eac6c30-4e06-4441-a84b-8019786ae69c . Variables such as employment status, income, current rating of the economy, education level, region etc (see above) were used as predictors in a partial proportional-odds model. Key findings are below:

  1. Best predictor of positive attitudes towards healthcare was access to healthcare itself. Feeling confident of getting good care was by far the strongest predictor: each step up the 4-point agreement scale multiplies the odds of a higher rating by 3.71. Rating the economy well comes second (1.41 per point), which suggests a general optimism about public institutions. Expecting the system to improve adds a smaller effect (1.20 per step).

  2. In terms of demographics, the location was the most important predictor variable. Quebecers have about half the odds of a higher rating than Ontarians (OR 0.52), and Albertans are also lower (OR 0.70), even after accounting for their attitudes.

  3. Women rate the system lower than men (OR 0.75), and so do people whose top priority for government is health care itself (OR 0.75). Those who want to prioritize it more than the economy are more likely to have more negative impressions of our healthcare system.

  4. Age and income affect how extreme a rating is: older Canadians are more likely to give the very lowest rating (OR 0.75 per decade for rating above 1). At the top of the scale, the effect flips direction (OR 1.10 for rating above 8), though that estimate is uncertain. Income works the other way, higher-income respondents are less likely to give the top ratings.

  5. Education, employment, having a family doctor and views on funding vs management show no clear effect once everything else is accounted for.

Analysis plan

The question is which characteristics and attitudes best explain how Canadians rate their health care system (rated 1 = terrible to 10 = excellent). Data was aquired from Open Canada dataset https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/3eac6c30-4e06-4441-a84b-8019786ae69c

The outcome is ordinal: the ratings have a clear order, but the distance between a 3 and a 4 need not equal the distance between an 8 and a 9. The model is therefore an partial proportional odds model, weighted with the survey weights so the results represent Canadians rather than just the people who answered.

Employment_status → employment Nominal Students (26) are merged with “not in workforce”.
Current_state_eco → econ_rating Numeric, 1–10 Same 1–10 scale as the outcome.
AGE → age10 Numeric Age in decades, so the odds ratio is per 10 years.

Weights
  • Survey data like this also has survey weights. Although I didn’t use it to adjust the variables (except provincial data above) during EDA, it’s important to consider this variable when designing statistical tests
    • The function of survey weight is to further control for sampling bias in a survey, particularly one that aims to make some sort of generalization about a country rather than the sample respondents
    • As all surveys may not represent the population exactly, some may be skewed with too many educated respondents, or too many cases sampled from the territories compared to largeer provinces (thus having a minority oversample!)
    • The weight value helps control for this
  • It is too much to display what each variable changes based on it’s weight so below is a quick summary table of what percentage shift we observed when using weighted variable adjustments vs unweighted
def weight_shift(df, cols, w='Weight'):
    out = {}
    for c in cols:
        if pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(df[c]):
            # continuous: shift in mean, in SD units
            d = df[[c, w]].dropna()
            wm = np.average(d[c], weights=d[w])
            out[c] = abs(wm - d[c].mean()) / d[c].std()
        else:
            # categorical/ordinal: largest shift in any category's proportion
            u = df[c].value_counts(normalize=True)
            wt = df.groupby(c)[w].sum() / df.loc[df[c].notna(), w].sum()
            out[c] = (wt - u).abs().max()
    return pd.Series(out).sort_values(ascending=False)

Check 1 · The outcome is ordinal and its categories are usable

The model estimates one threshold between each pair of neighbouring categories (9 thresholds for a 1–10 scale). A threshold next to a nearly empty category is poorly estimated and can stop the model from converging. If there are empty or very sparse edge categories, we can merge them (for example, 1–2 into “1–2”).

Check 2 · Enough respondents in each predictor category

Each level of a nominal predictor gets its own coefficient. A level with few respondents gives a wide, unstable estimate, and empty level × outcome cells can make the proportional-odds tests fail. To address this, we can merge levels that don’t pass into a sensible neighbour. This has already been done for provinces, government focus and employment. These are usually sparse categories, or high-cardinality variables (lots of unique values) that can be grouped into matching themes.

Check 3 · No severe multicollinearity

When predictors overlap strongly (for example, age and being retired), their coefficients can have inflated standard errors, and the model can’t separate their effects. That undermines the question of which variable best explains the outcome, since both variables could be explaining the same thing.

Collinearity depends only on the predictors, so the generalised VIF from a linear model with the same right-hand side is a valid check. For factors with more than one degree of freedom, GVIF^(1/(2·Df)) is the comparable figure. Values above about 2.2 (√5) deserve attention.

If it fails we can: drop or combine one of the overlapping predictors, or interpret them together as a block.

Check 4 · Proportional odds (parallel lines)

This is the key assumption of the model. It asks whether each predictor has the same effect at every threshold of the outcome: the odds ratio for “rating ≥ 3 vs ≤ 2” should equal the odds ratio for “rating ≥ 9 vs ≤ 8”. If a predictor mainly separates people who rate the system terribly from everyone else, one coefficient averages over effects that really differ.

We can check this in two ways:

  1. Graphical: empirical cumulative logits by predictor level. We’re looking for roughly parallel lines within each predictor.
  2. Formal test: ordinal::nominal_test(), a likelihood-ratio test that relaxes the assumption one term at a time.

If it fails: for the predictors that clearly violate it, use a partial proportional-odds model, which lets those predictors have threshold-specific effects (clm(..., nominal = ~ x)).

4a · Cumulative logits by group

Code 
cum_logit_data <- function(data, v) {
  data |>
    count(level = .data[[v]], hc_rating) |>
    group_by(level) |>
    mutate(p_le = cumsum(n) / sum(n)) |>
    ungroup() |>
    filter(hc_rating != "10") |>
    mutate(variable = v,
           threshold = as.integer(as.character(hc_rating)),
           logit = qlogis(pmin(pmax(p_le, 0.01), 0.99)))
}

cum_logit_plot <- function(d, ncol = 2) {

    theme_minimal() +
    theme(legend.position = "bottom", legend.text = element_text(size = 7))
}

4c · Age and income

To draw age and income the same way, they’re split into groups: age into quartiles, income into low, middle and high brackets.

Code

(cum_logit_data(hc_grouped, "age") |> cum_logit_plot(ncol = 1)) |
  (cum_logit_data(hc_grouped, "income") |> cum_logit_plot(ncol = 1))

Neither of these is parallel:

  • Age: the youngest group (18–42) sits well below the others at threshold 1, so few young people rate the system a 1. The oldest group (over 64) drops away at thresholds 7–8, so more older people give an 8 or higher. Older respondents are more likely to sit at both ends of the scale.
  • Income: the lowest-income line (under $40k) is flatter than the others. It sits higher at threshold 1 and lower at threshold 9, so lower-income respondents give more ratings at both extremes, and higher-income respondents cluster in the middle.

Check fails for age and income. With a single coefficient, these opposite effects at the two ends of the scale would average out to roughly zero, and the model would wrongly report “no effect”. The final model therefore uses a partial proportional-odds model: age and income get a separate effect at each threshold, and every other predictor keeps one coefficient.

3 · Model and results

The model is fitted with ordinal::clm(), with age and income as threshold-specific (nominal) effects. The survey weights are rescaled to average 1 so they reweight respondents without inflating the sample size.

Code 
hc <- hc |> mutate(w = weight / mean(weight))

f_loc <- hc_rating ~ region + gender + education + employment + fam_doc +
  econ_rating + access_conf + future_outlook + hc_needs + gov_focus

fit <- clm(f_loc, nominal = ~ age10 + income, data = hc, weights = w)

Which factors matter most?

Each predictor is dropped from the model in turn, and a likelihood-ratio test measures how much worse the fit gets. The bigger the χ², the more that predictor explains, given everything else in the model.

Code 
loc_terms <- attr(terms(f_loc), "term.labels")
nom_terms <- c("age10", "income")

importance <- map_dfr(c(loc_terms, nom_terms), \(t) {
  reduced <- if (t %in% nom_terms) {
    clm(f_loc, nominal = reformulate(setdiff(nom_terms, t)), data = hc, weights = w)
  } else {
    clm(update(f_loc, as.formula(paste(". ~ . -", t))),
        nominal = ~ age10 + income, data = hc, weights = w)
  }
  a <- anova(reduced, fit)
  tibble(term = t, chisq = a$LR.stat[2], df = a$df[2], p = a$`Pr(>Chisq)`[2])
}) |>
  arrange(p)

Key findings

Code 
or <- \(t) sprintf("%.2f", exp(fit$beta[[t]]))
or_nom <- \(t) sprintf("%.2f", exp(-fit$alpha[[t]]))

  1. Attitudes matter far more than demographics. Feeling confident of getting good care if seriously ill is by far the strongest predictor: each step up the 4-point agreement scale multiplies the odds of a higher rating by 3.71. Rating the economy well comes second (1.41 per point), which suggests a general optimism about public institutions. Expecting the system to improve adds a smaller effect (1.20 per step).

  2. Region is the strongest demographic factor. Quebecers have about half the odds of a higher rating than Ontarians (OR 0.52), and Albertans are also lower (OR 0.70), even after accounting for their attitudes.

  3. Women rate the system lower than men (OR 0.75), and so do people whose top priority for government is health care itself (OR 0.75).

  4. Age and income affect how extreme a rating is, not just its level. Older Canadians are clearly more likely to give the very lowest rating (OR 0.75 per decade for rating above 1). At the top of the scale the effect flips direction (OR 1.10 for rating above 8), though that estimate is uncertain. Income works the other way round: higher-income respondents are less likely to give the top ratings. A standard proportional-odds model would have averaged these effects to roughly zero.

  5. Education, employment, having a family doctor and views on funding vs management show no clear effect once everything else is accounted for.

This analysis uses cross-sectional survey data, so these are associations, not causes.
